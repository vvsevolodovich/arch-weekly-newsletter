## SPOTIFY'S AI RELIABILITY PROBLEM

**THE PROBLEM**

You've probably had this conversation. AI velocity metrics are up — PRs merged, features shipped, tickets closed. Leadership is happy. And then six months in, you're in an incident retrospective trying to figure out how a regression that would have been caught in a normal review cycle made it to production.

The Pragmatic Engineer documented four Spotify outages in fourteen months. May 2026: over 30,000 Downdetector reports. A 90-minute disruption in June. A July 14th platform failure that didn't appear on Spotify's own status page — customers were reporting failures while the dashboard showed green.

**WHAT THEY DID**

This isn't a post-mortem Spotify published. What Pragmatic Engineer documented is the correlation from the outside: 99% AI adoption claims and a 76% PR frequency increase running alongside a clear reliability regression. No causal analysis from Spotify's engineering team — just the pattern, visible to everyone.

**MY TAKE**

Here's the structural problem: AI-assisted pipelines amplify whatever your weakest developer would do unchecked. If your review culture was already thin, if your test coverage already had gaps, if your observability already had blind spots — AI velocity makes all of that worse, faster. A 76% PR frequency increase means 76% more defect surface. Verification investment didn't scale proportionally, and you can see the results on Downdetector.

The status page staying green during a real outage is a separate and arguably worse failure — your observability is lying at exactly the moment it matters most.

**TAKEAWAY**

Before you celebrate your AI-driven velocity metrics, pull your incident rate for the same period. If velocity went up and reliability went down, you are not moving faster — you are borrowing against your reliability budget.

## STRIPE'S SELF-HEALING DATABASE FLEET

**THE PROBLEM**

You've probably hand-held through a database incident. The alert fires, the on-call engineer wakes up, spends 20 minutes piecing together cluster state from a dozen dashboards, and then manually sequences the recovery steps — against a runbook that's months out of date for the actual topology.

At Stripe's scale: 2,000+ MongoDB shards handling 5 million QPS. That process was generating around 200 alert pages per year. Shards were sitting in unhealthy states for a cumulative 12 days annually — not 12 days of downtime, but 12 days of degraded state accumulating across thousands of shards throughout the year.

**WHAT THEY DID**

Stripe modeled their entire shard topology as a traversable graph. Each shard state is a node; valid state transitions are edges. When a shard enters a bad state, BFS finds the minimum-step path back to healthy, and a finite state machine enforces which transitions are legal at each step.

What's elegant: they didn't hard-code any recovery procedures. New shard layouts require zero changes to recovery logic — remediation derives from the graph. Results: pager volume down 30%, all 12 accumulated days of annual unhealthy shard state eliminated.

**MY TAKE**

Most automated runbook systems hard-code procedures, which means every topology change breaks the automation. This approach breaks that coupling — the graph is the source of truth. The FSM gives you safety: no invalid transitions. BFS gives you optimality: shortest path, not just any path.

The failure mode I'd push on: graph-based pathfinding assumes your control plane's view of shard state is accurate. Under a network partition, the control plane's state model may diverge from actual shard state — and BFS will confidently navigate toward the wrong destination.

**TAKEAWAY**

Your operational runbook is already a graph — you just haven't made it executable. If you run any database fleet at scale, model the state machine. Don't wait for the next incident to force the conversation.

## NETFLIX BETS ON vLLM

**THE PROBLEM**

You've probably felt the pull to build specialized infrastructure for every new model type. XGBoost in production? One serving path. PyTorch? Another. LLMs arrive, and the instinct is to give them their own stack — dedicated infrastructure, dedicated on-call, dedicated scaling policy. Six months later, you have four serving systems, none of them well-maintained, and operational overhead is eating your ML roadmap.

**WHAT THEY DID**

Netflix already had the Model Scoring Service — a unified serving layer over Triton for XGBoost, TensorFlow, and PyTorch. When LLMs arrived, they evaluated vLLM against TensorRT-LLM and routed the winner through the same Triton backend. Same Java control plane. Same versioning. Same multi-region rollout infrastructure.

They chose vLLM for four specific reasons: loading custom architectures without a compilation pipeline, extensibility hooks for custom decoding logic, better debuggability, and practitioner familiarity inside the ML org. Not raw performance — by summer 2025, open-source engines had closed the gap with specialized stacks on every metric that mattered.

**MY TAKE**

The interesting call isn't vLLM versus TensorRT-LLM — it's absorbing LLMs into the existing unified serving path rather than giving them their own stack. LLMs inherit Netflix's existing investment in versioning, scaling, and rollout infrastructure. That's the right architectural bet when you already have working infrastructure.

The tradeoff worth naming: autoregressive decoding has fundamentally different throughput characteristics than a forward pass through XGBoost. Unified infrastructure means shared resource pools, and shared pools mean noisy-neighbor dynamics between model types.

**TAKEAWAY**

When you're adding a new model type, ask whether your existing serving infrastructure can absorb it before you build something net-new. The operational cost of a second system is almost always higher than you estimate going in.

## UBER'S GIT-AS-A-SERVICE

**THE PROBLEM**

You've probably never waited 15 minutes for a git clone in your terminal — but your CI pipeline almost certainly has. At Uber's scale, every automation system touching the monorepo — code-review validation, security scanning, audit PR generation — was spending up to 15 minutes just getting the repository into a usable state before doing any actual work. Multiply that across hundreds of automation systems running continuously and you have a massive compute sink producing nothing productive.

**WHAT THEY DID**

Uber built GitFarm: a gRPC API that executes Git commands against pre-warmed bare repository clones held as ephemeral sandboxes. You call a standard Git command via API; GitFarm pulls a warm sandbox from its pool in under 500ms, executes the operation in isolated ephemeral storage, and destroys the sandbox on completion.

Numbers: 80%+ reduction in resource overhead, 70%+ reduction in client-side resource utilization, initialization time down from up to 15 minutes to under 1 minute.

**MY TAKE**

The bottleneck was never Git itself — it was every automation client reproducing the same expensive initial state independently from scratch. GitFarm inverts the model: maintain warm state on the server, not the client. This is the same pattern as connection pooling in databases. Applied to monorepo automation, it's the correct call.

The tradeoff: you've centralized that warm state, and the pool becomes a single point of failure for all automated workflows. Stripe-level database resilience thinking now applies to your Git infrastructure.

**TAKEAWAY**

If you have a monorepo and automation running against it at any meaningful frequency, audit your clone cost first. The server-side warm state model is the right architecture — the constraint is the infrastructure investment to build it.

## POSTGRES 19 REPACK

**THE PROBLEM**

You've probably hit this. Large Postgres table, months of writes, MVCC dead tuples eating disk and slowing scans. The standard fix — VACUUM FULL — reclaims the space but holds an ACCESS EXCLUSIVE lock for the entire table rewrite. Hours of zero reads and zero writes. In any production system with SLA requirements, that's not a real option.

The community workaround has been pg_repack, a third-party extension that rewrites concurrently. But extensions are fragile — compiled against a specific Postgres version, requiring separate installation, historically prone to breaking on major version upgrades.

**WHAT THEY DID**

Postgres 19 ships REPACK natively. REPACK CONCURRENTLY rewrites the table under a shared update exclusive lock — the same tier as regular VACUUM — and acquires the exclusive lock only briefly at the final swap. Full table rewrite, traffic continues throughout.

Caveats that matter: doesn't work on unlogged tables, partitioned tables, or tables without a primary key or replica identity. Beta 2 shipped July 16; GA expected September/October 2026.

**MY TAKE**

This is pg_repack getting absorbed into core, which is exactly how Postgres should evolve. The extension proves the capability, the community formalizes it. The minimal exclusive lock window at the final swap is the same technique pg_repack pioneered — validated prior art landing in the official implementation. The caveat about partitioned tables is the real constraint for modern schemas; most teams creating large tables today are reaching for partitioning first.

**TAKEAWAY**

If you're running Postgres and have any table larger than a few GB, REPACK CONCURRENTLY is the feature that justifies the Postgres 19 upgrade. Check whether your large tables have primary keys and replica identities set before you plan the migration.

## POST-QUANTUM SIGNATURES NOW

**THE PROBLEM**

You've heard post-quantum cryptography is coming. Maybe you've heard about ML-KEM for key exchange and assumed you have time to figure out signatures later. NIST has nine new post-quantum signature candidates still in evaluation, and waiting for a better option than what we have today sounds reasonable. Here's the problem: those candidates won't be standardized until 2030 at the earliest. Federal mandates require post-quantum readiness before that deadline. You don't have the runway you think you have.

**WHAT THEY DID**

Cloudflare's argument is straightforward: ML-DSA is what we have, deploy it now. The numbers that make this uncomfortable — ML-DSA signatures run 2,420 to 4,595 bytes against ECDSA's 64 bytes. That's 38 to 72 times larger on the wire. At that size, roughly 5% of TLS connections fail outright on middlebox-heavy networks, and the rest slow down.

The migration architecture is asymmetric: ML-DSA replaces ECDSA for authentication, while ML-KEM handles key agreement. ML-KEM already covers more than half of human-initiated web traffic through Cloudflare as of late 2025.

**MY TAKE**

A 38 to 72x signature size increase causing 5% connection failures is a real cost that most organizations haven't modeled yet. Cloudflare's argument is temporal — the alternative to an imperfect solution now is no compliant solution by the mandate deadline — and that argument is correct.

What they're glossing over: enterprise middlebox-heavy environments may face significantly higher than 5% failure rates. Cloudflare's baseline comes from consumer traffic distributions. If your users are behind deep packet inspection, strict firewalls, or aging network appliances, audit your specific failure rate before you're forced to migrate under deadline pressure.

**TAKEAWAY**

Model what ML-DSA's signature size does to your TLS connection success rate before your mandate deadline, not during implementation. 5% is Cloudflare's traffic baseline. Yours may be different, and you need to know that now.

## AGENTIC TESTING AT SLACK

**THE PROBLEM**

You've probably been tempted to replace brittle Playwright scripts with AI agents. The pitch is obvious: instead of maintaining test code that breaks every time the UI changes, you point an agent at the app and say "verify that a user can send a message." The agent figures out the mechanics. No more test maintenance overhead.

The question nobody answered clearly until now: what do the actual failure rates look like at production scale?

**WHAT THEY DID**

Slack ran 200+ agentic E2E workflows across three approaches and published the numbers:
- Playwright MCP on complex flows: 0–12% failure rate
- Playwright CLI: 12–20% failure rate
- Agent-generated Playwright scripts on simple/complex workflows: ~8% and ~48% respectively

Cost: $15–30 per run. Execution time: 10+ minutes per workflow.

**MY TAKE**

The 48% failure rate on complex agent-generated scripts is the number to sit with. That's a coin flip on your most important user flows. Slack's architectural conclusion is correct: agentic testing is not a CI regression gate. It's a specialized exploratory coverage tool for cases where UI churn makes deterministic tests impractical to maintain.

At $15–30 per run, the cost at any meaningful CI frequency compounds fast. This is not a drop-in replacement for your test suite — it's an expensive, specialized instrument for a narrow problem. Budget it accordingly, or you'll have convinced yourself you improved coverage while burning money on unreliable results.

**TAKEAWAY**

Keep deterministic Playwright scripts as your regression gate. Add agents selectively for goal-oriented validation on critical flows where UI churn has made test maintenance genuinely unsustainable. Count the cost per run before you commit to scale.
